The Monty Hall Problem

1 Introduction

In 1963, a game show called Let’s make a deal started in the USA. The host was the famous Monty Hall. On the show, Monty proposed a problem in probability that confuses the layman even today. The mathematician, on the other hand, might be able to understand the solution with a probability of \(\frac23\)! We also explore some other variations of the classical problem, and the optimal strategies to maximise the payoff, some of which may help in developing the intuition behind the original problem.

2 Monty Hall: The Classical Problem

Suppose your friend Monty Hall takes you in front of three closed doors \(D_1, D_2, D_3\). Behind one of them is a car and behind the two others are goats. You do not know what is behind which door, but Monty knows. So, he asks you to choose a door. Out of the two remaining doors, he opens one door which doesn’t have a car behind it. Now, should you stick to your original choice, or should you switch to the other unopened door in order to maximise your chances of winning a car?

2.1 Incorrect solution

One flawed argument that wrongly solves this problem goes as: After Monty opens the door which has a goat, one of the remaining two doors has a car and other one has a goat. So he has \(\frac{1}{2}\) probability of getting a car in either door. So, it doesn’t matter whether he switches or not.

But, actually, it does. Switching gives you a \(\frac{2}{3}\) probability of winning. But sticking gives you a probability of \(\frac{1}{3}\) of winning. This can be explained as follows:

2.2 Correct solution

We have to calculate the conditonal probability that car is behind our originally selected door given that car is not behind a particular other door (i.e. the one that Monty has opened). Suppose, without loss of any generality, that I have picked door \(D_3\) initially, and Monty has opened \(D_2\).

\(P\left({\text{Car behind }D_3}\mid{\text{Monty opens }D_2}\right) = \frac{P\left(\text{Monty opens }D_2\ \cap\text{ Car behind }D_3\right)}{P\left(\text{Monty opens }D_2\right)}\)

In order to compute this, we would have to look at multiple cases on where the car is and what door you pick initially. We do this by treating our problem statistically:

2.2.1 Statistical treatment

One way to look at probability is statistics. So, we iterate this experiment \(N\) times, and look at how many times you get the car by switching or sticking respectively.

There is one-third probability of the car being behind any door. So, in \(\frac{N}{3}\) times, the car would be behind \(D_1\). Consider these only for now. We talk for these \(\frac{N}{9}\) only for now.

You also have one-third probability of picking any particular door initially. So, \(\frac{N}{9}\) times, you pick \(D_1\); \(\frac{N}{9}\) times, you pick \(D_2\); \(\frac{N}{9}\) times, you pick \(D_3\).

Take the first case: you pick \(D_1\). Goats behind \(D_2\) and \(D_3\). So, \(\frac{N}{9}\) times, Monty opens either \(D_2\) or \(D_3\). You must stick to get car.

Take the second case: you pick \(D_2\). Goats behind \(D_2, D_3\). All \(\frac{N}{9}\) times, Monty must open \(D_3\). You must switch to get the car.

Take the third case: this is symmetric to the second case. All \(\frac{N}{9}\) times, you must switch to get the car.

Now, out of the \(\frac{N}{3}\) times, you must switch \(\frac{N}{9}+\frac{N}{9}=\frac{2N}{9}\) times and stick \(\frac{N}{18}+\frac{N}{18}=\frac{N}{9}\) times.

The other iterations out of \(N\), where car is behind the door \(D_2\) and \(D_3\) are symmetrical. So, to get the car, you must: \(\text{Switch} = \frac{2N}{3} \text{ times}\) and \(\text{Stick} = \frac{N}{3} \text{ times}\)

So, probabilities are \(\frac23\) and \(\frac13\), respectively.

If we assign a car 1 point, and a goat 0 point, so your expected payoff on switching would be \(\frac23\) and on sticking would be \(\frac13\).

The erroneous argument that leads to the expected payoff of \(\frac12\) is because of poorly or incorrectly defined sample spaces.

Let’s look at another variation of the Monty Hall problem.

3 Monty Hall, you, Bob and Marley

This time, your friends Bob and Marley are also here for the car, just like you. They, too, don’t know where the car is. So, Monty asks all of you to pick different doors simultaneously (assume that if any two of you pick the same door, that choice is cancelled, and all of you pick again, and this process repeats until all three don’t pick different doors). Then, Monty instructs any one (each with probability \(\frac12\)), who Monty knows has picked a door with a goat, to open the door. That person is eliminated. Then Monty gives the two survivors the option to switch or stick (each one unaware of what the other person decides). If one person chooses to stick and one person chooses to switch, then each person gets half points of what is behind the common selected door. After that, we look at what the optimal strategies of the remaining two players should be, assuming Nash equilbrium conditions.

In game theory, the Nash equilibrium is a state where all the players are in their best individual states possible.

3.1 Analysis

Initially, the probability of your door not having a car is \(\frac23\). If you have a goat, initially, there is a \(\frac12\) chance of you getting eliminated. In these \(\frac13^{\text{rd}}\) cases, payoff is \(0\). Looking at the remaining \(\frac23^{\text{rd}}\) cases, where you survive the initial elimination, we construct a payoff table listing different scenarios. For this, we need to calculate the payoff for both swtich or both stick case.

Suppose WLOG, that \(D_2\) has been opened to reveal a goat.

To avoid getting all messy, we use conditional probability.

\(P(D_1 \text{ has the car} \mid D_2 \text{ has a goat}) = \frac{P(D_1 \text{ has the car } \cap D_2 \text{ has a goat})}{P(D_2 \text{ has a goat})} = \frac{\frac13}{\frac23}=\frac12\)

Symmetrically,

\(P(D_3 \text{ has the car} \mid D_2 \text{ has a goat}) = \frac{P(D_3 \text{ has the car } \cap D_2 \text{ has a goat})}{P(D_2 \text{ has a goat})} = \frac{\frac13}{\frac23}=\frac12\)

So, both of the two remaining doors are equally likely to have a car in this scenario.

This is an important distinction between the classical version and this version with Monty + three players. In the classical version, these probabilities turn out to be \(\frac{2}{3}\) for the door initially not selected by the person, and \(\frac13\) for the door initially selected by the person.

Now, if both of you decide to stick, then expected payoff for both of you (given that both of you survive the initial elimination) will be \(\frac12 \times 0 + \frac12 \times 1 = \frac12\) each.

If both of you decide to switch, then again the expected payoff for both of you (given that both of you survive the initial elimination) will be \(\frac12 \times 0 + \frac12 \times 1 = \frac12\) each.

Suppose if one decides to stay and one decides to switch, then the expected payoff for both of you (given that both of you survive the initial elimination) will be \(\frac12 \times 0 + \frac12 \times \frac12= \frac14\) each.

Therefore, there are two positions that correspond to a Nash equilibra: either both switch or both stick.

Assuming both play optimally, there is no reason to prefer switching over sticking or vice versa. So, suppose unideal behaviour and suppose that the other person has a probability \(p\) of sticking. You stick with probability \(q\). Then, the expected payoff over the whole game for you would be

\(\mathbb{E} = \frac13 \times 0 + \frac23 \times \left((1-p)(1-q)\times\frac12+(1-p)q\times\frac14+p(1-q)\times\frac14+pq\times\frac12\right) = \frac{(2p-1)q+(2-p)}{6}\)

This function is linear in \(p\).

This would be maximised if \(q=1\) (always stick) when \(p>\frac12\) and if \(q=0\) when \(p<\frac12\).

This should make sense, as you would try to match the other player’s move. If he is more likely to switch, you would switch, and vice versa.

4 Infinity Hall

New scenario: There are \(n\) doors \(n\in\mathbb{N}\ \ n \geq3\) such that there is a car (carries 1 point) behind exactly one of these doors and goats (each goat carries 0 points) behind all other doors. You select any one door. Then Monty opens \(n-2\) doors that have goats. What is your expected score?

4.1 Incorrect solution for \(n\) doors

After revealing the goats behind \(n-2\) doors, we have that there are two doors left, and there’s a car behind either one of them. So, it must be that one door has a car and one a goat. So, expected payoff should be \(\mathbb{E} = \frac12 \times 1 + \frac12 \times 0 =\frac12\).

But it is incorrect. Intuitively, if Monty has opened all but two doors, out of which the one you picked he’s bound by the rules to not open, the other unopened door probably has the car.

Let’s see the correct solution and extrapolate the result to an infinite number of doors.

4.2 Correct solution

We consider two mutually exclusive cases:

Case 1: The initial choice has a goat. Probability of this event is \(\frac{n-1}{n}\). You must switch to win.

Case 2: The initial choice has a car. Probability of this event is \(\frac{1}{n}\). You must stick to win.

So clearly, probability of winning a car on switching is \(\frac{n-1}{n}\) and probability of winning a car on sticking is \(\frac1n\). So, following the most optimal strategy, you should always switch. Thus, your expected payoff = \(\mathbb{E} = \frac{n-1}{n}\times1+\frac1n\times0=\frac{n-1}{n}\)

4.3 \(\lim_{n\to \infty}{\mathbb{E}}\)

This limit trivially turns out to be \(\lim_{n\to \infty}{\frac{n-1}{n}} = 1\).

So, while playing against Infinity Hall, switching almost always guarantees a car.

5 Darth Hall: Monty is evil

Frustrated that you have cracked the classical version, Monty has now turned to the Dark side. He modifies the game, and is now trying to make you lose. Now, he imposes a penalty on you every time you switch.

New rules: A car carries 1 point. A goat carries \(0\) points. Now, switching results in a penalty of \(p\) points. You have to find the optimal strategy to maximise your score.

5.1 Solution

We construct a payoff table, listing various scenarios, and how much you would gain from them.

Car is behind door (only Monty knows): \(D_1\) \(D_2\) \(D_3\)
The door you pick initially, and what you do
\(D_1\), switch \(-p\) \(1-p\) \(1-p\)
\(D_2\), switch \(1-p\) \(-p\) \(1-p\)
\(D_3\), switch \(1-p\) \(1-p\) \(-p\)
\(D_1\), stick \(1\) \(0\) \(0\)
\(D_2\), stick \(0\) \(1\) \(0\)
\(D_3\), stick \(0\) \(0\) \(1\)

Suppose now that you stick to your original choice with probabilty \(s\). Probability of car actually being behind door \(D_1\), \(D_2\) or \(D_3\) is \(\frac13\) each. Proability of intially selecting any particular door is also \(\frac13\), as when all are closed, you can’t tell the difference between any two. Then, expected payoff is

\(\mathbb{E}=3\times\frac13\times\frac13\left(s\times0 +(1-s)(1-p)+s\times0+(1-s)(1-p) + s\times1 + (1-s)(1-p)\right) = s\left(p-\frac13\right)+\left(\frac23-p\right)\)

For a fixed \(p\), \(\mathbb{E}\) is a linear function of \(s\). We want to maximise \(\mathbb{E}\).

5.1.1 Final answer

If \(p>\frac13\), then it is favourable to always stick \(s=1\), as the penalty is too high. If \(p<\frac13\), then it is favourable to switch \(s=0\). For \(p=\frac13\), it does not matter if you switch or stick, as \(\mathbb{E} = \frac13\).

Considering a special case, where \(p=0\), that is the OG version, we decide to switch. Thus, we get \(\mathbb{E} = \frac23\), which makes sense.

6 Philosophy of the Monty Hall problem

As has been seen above, the Monty Hall problem seems to become insanely difficult only if you don’t define the sample spaces properly, or if you erroneously redefine them in between the experiment.

Someone without a complete knowledge of the correct solution might link it with Bertrand’s paradox, whose result is effectively that the different, but none wrong, random variables may lead to different probabilities of the same thing. But in our case of the Monty Hall problem, there is no such thing.

We can relate Bayesian probability with statistical probability, as we did for the solution of the classical version.

6.1 Further extensions

Several other variations are also possible! For example, we have the quantum version of this problem, where the your and Monty’s actions, as well as the states of the doors may be stored in wavefunctions, “kets”. These are represented as \(|\psi\rangle\). Using various logical gates on the doors can be used to contruct the W-state, where 3 qubits are maximally entangled. The W state is

\(|W\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt3}\left(|001\rangle+|010\rangle+|100\rangle\right)\)
